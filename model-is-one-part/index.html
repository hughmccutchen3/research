TL;DR

What people call “AI” is a system. The is the part everyone talks about, but focusing on the model is like talking about a car and discussing only its engine. The model takes text in and gives text out, one step at a time, and keeps nothing from one step to the next. That is true of every system built on a large language model [1, 2, 3]. Much of what matters is built around the model, to help us use it well and safely.

The layer closest to the model is the : the program that calls the model, decides what it gets to see, carries out the actions it asks for, and stops the at a , such as a maximum number of turns or a spending cap [4, 5]. The harness works inside an , which sets what a run can reach: which files, which networks, which other systems [6, 7]. , the settings chosen by whoever sets up a deployment, decide how much can happen without a person saying yes first [8, 9]. watch from outside and can raise an alert or stop a run. Above all of it sits : the people and policies that own each of those settings.

While a model is being trained and tested, the company doing the owns all of those layers and their governance, and it sets them up differently from the way its customers will use the model. Once customers use it, who controls what depends on how the model is reached. In a chat app, the company providing it runs the harness and writes the instructions the model is given [10, 11]. In a coding assistant, through the API, or with open downloaded and run on your own machines, more of the system is yours to set [4, 8, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

BURSERA’S TAKE — THE WORDS THAT SPAN A MASSIVE TOPICBursera’s own view — not the record

Right now the commentary and news put one label, “AI”, on an incredibly complex, multi-layered set of technologies. Most of the media energy goes to the “frontier models”. What we hear from the AI companies is mostly about new models with clever names, “agents” bundled up to make our lives easier, or, most ambitious of all, a superintelligence that can out-think and outsmart any human.

The same few words, “AI”, “model” and “agent”, carry all of it: new mathematics, a restricted model reached by people who were not meant to have it, agents probing public websites, new ways of working and unparalleled job disruption. And there is always the “Skynet” level of fear and references to a “mass extinction event”.

For normal consumers our greatest risk is bad information. But it really helps to understand how the whole thing works. Without the right controls around the whole system, it can do unexpected things, and some of them can be bad.

1 · The parts of an “AI” system like ChatGPT or Claude

Figure 1 is the map for the rest of this paper. This section takes the parts one at a time: what a model is, what it is trained on, what does not change when you use it, and everything that sits around it [4, 6, 26, 27].

Key idea 1

“AI” is a system, not a model.

The model is one part. Around it sit the harness, the tools, the execution environment, the controls, and the governance that sets them. When an AI system does something surprising, any of these parts can be the reason.

1.1 What a model is

A model is a very large set of numbers, called weights or parameters. GPT-3, for example, has 175 billion of them [28]. Imagine those are all dials that are tuned to make the model work the way it does (Further reading 1). The picture helps if two things are kept in mind. First, no single weight means anything a person could name: there is no creativity dial and no accuracy dial. Researchers at both companies report that each idea is spread across many parts of the network, and each part helps with many ideas [29, 30]. Second, no person sets the weights by hand. A training algorithm adjusts them, in many small steps, toward better scores (Further reading 1 and 4).

The people training the model choose what counts as better: the data, the goal and the feedback [31].

Training happens in stages [28, 31]. First comes pretraining: the model reads a very large body of text and is adjusted, over and over, to get better at predicting the next , the next small piece of text [28]. That gives it a broad command of language and a great deal of knowledge, but no particular aim. Next, it is tuned on examples of good answers written by people [31]. Then comes reinforcement learning: the model makes attempts, each attempt is scored, and the weights move toward higher scores. The score can come from people’s preferences, from another model judging the answer against a written set of principles, or from automatic checks of whether the answer is right [31, 32, 33]. OpenAI says its reasoning models are trained to reason this way [34]; Anthropic describes pretraining followed by “substantial post-training”, including reinforcement learning from human and from AI feedback [35].

Both companies also train toward published documents that describe how a model should behave: Anthropic’s constitution and OpenAI’s Model Spec [36, 37]. Training toward a document shapes the weights before the model is ever used. That is different from the described in section 1.4, which check each request while the model is in use. Both companies do both [35, 37].

Labs check whether training worked by evaluating the model: test sets, human ratings, red-teaming, and graders that score its attempts. An evaluation measures only what it tests; OpenAI writes that “Capability claims are only as strong as the elicitation behind them” [38].

Key idea 2

A model is a fixed set of numbers.

Its many billions of weights are set by training on trillions of tokens of text, and do not change while it is used. No person sets them by hand, and no single one means anything you could name.

1.2 What the model is trained on

What content goes into the training is a choice. A lab draws on several sources of text for what each adds, and each source comes with limits that differ from one organization to the next [35, 39, 40]. Neither OpenAI nor Anthropic says how much of its training data comes from each source. Figure 1b shows the sources.

Filtering and mixing apply to every source. Each lab decides what to remove, such as spam, hate speech, adult content and duplicates, and how often to draw on each source; GPT-3, for example, drew more often on the data its makers judged higher quality [28, 34, 35, 39].

Key idea 3

The data used in training is a choice.

Each lab decides what goes in, what is filtered out and how much of each source to use. The choices, and the limits on them, differ from lab to lab.

These choices produce different kinds of models. The largest models are trained for breadth, on the mix in the table above. Smaller models trade breadth for focus: Microsoft trained Phi-3-mini, a model small enough to run on a phone, on heavily filtered web data and synthetic data [55]. Meta releases its Llama 3 models and says it trained them on about 15 trillion tokens, but not how much came from each source [56]. Ai2 releases its OLMo models together with their full training data, the open Dolma corpus, and publishes how much of it comes from each source, so anyone can inspect what went in [57]. The range runs from breadth and capability at one end to focus, smaller hardware and openness at the other.

Even for developers calling the largest models through the API, the model itself now has very few settings that can be changed on each request, and the labs are removing some of them. Temperature, which controls how random a model’s word choices are, was long the standard one [58]. Anthropic’s documentation says temperature and the related settings “are not supported on Claude 4.7 and later models” [3]; OpenAI tells developers to remove them from requests to its newest models whenever the model is set to reason [59]. What you can shape now sits mostly in the harness: the instructions and skills it adds to the , the it allows and the around them.

1.3 What does not change when you use the model

When you use a model in a chat app or to write code, nothing you do changes its weights. That answers a common question: does the chatbot learn from me as I talk? The model does not [28]. What changes is the context, described in section 1.4: the conversation and any saved memory, which the app sends back to the model every time [60]. Your chats can change a future model only if they are used to train it [45]. Each request starts fresh, with the whole conversation sent again [1, 3]; OpenAI’s API can also keep earlier responses on its side, so a conversation can continue from them [1].

Key idea 4

Using a model does not change it.

What changes is the context: everything sent to the model on each call. Memory, projects and custom instructions all work by adding to the context.

1.4 What sits around the model

The user interface is the part each of us sees. In a chat, text goes back and forth between your screen and the model behind it. Many products add more: projects, skills, custom instructions, profiles, memory. None of these changes the model. Everything you see and adjust in the interface changes what is sent to the model: the context.

The harness is the program behind the interface that runs the model. Anthropic calls it “the layer around the model that provides the tools and manages the context the model sees” [4]. It does four jobs: it assembles what the model reads, carries out the actions the model asks for, enforces limits such as a maximum number of turns, and keeps a record of the run, the [4, 5]. OpenAI’s tools work the same way: a run can pause for a person to approve an action, and the number of turns can be capped [14, 61].

Key idea 5

The harness manages the model.

It decides what the model sees, carries out the actions the model asks for, and stops the run at a limit.

Context is everything the model is shown on one call: the instructions, the conversation so far, and the results of any actions. It grows with every turn, up to a size limit, the context window. When a run gets close to that limit, the harness compacts the context, summarizing or clearing older material so the run can continue [4, 5, 62]. The unit of context is the token: a small piece of text, such as a word or part of a word [58, 63].

Tools are the actions the harness can take for the model: reading and editing files, running commands, searching the web, connecting to other services [4]. The model can only ask for a tool. The harness decides whether the request goes ahead, and runs it [4, 14].

The execution environment is where the tools run and what they can reach: files, networks, other services [6, 7]. It might be your laptop, a company server or the vendor’s cloud [4, 64]. A sandbox is a fence, enforced by the operating system, around that environment. Products set sandboxes up differently: Claude Code’s sandbox is off until you turn it on, while Codex starts its main agent inside one [6, 7].

Instructions and controls are different things, and the difference matters. An instruction, such as a system prompt or a project file, is text in the context: the model may follow it. A control is enforced outside the model, whatever the model proposes. Anthropic’s documentation puts it plainly: memory files are “context, not enforced configuration” [60].

Key idea 6

Instructions are not controls.

An instruction is text the model may follow. A control is enforced outside the model, whatever the model proposes.

Model safeguards come with the model: refusals it was trained to give, plus checks the vendor runs on requests and responses, with warnings, throttling or suspension for misuse [26, 27, 65].

Monitors watch a run from outside and can raise an alert or stop it. The transcript is not a monitor: it records the run but watches nothing [4, 7, 26].

Governance sits above all of it: the people and policies that set and own the controls on every layer, training and the model safeguards included. Both companies have said that some of their own evaluations ran with safeguards switched off, a governance choice [10, 66]. Together, the controls are everything outside the model that bounds a run: the harness’s limits, the execution environment’s reach, operator controls, model safeguards and monitors. An agent is the model, the harness and the execution environment working on a task together.

2 · Who runs which layer depends on how you reach the model

The parts in Figure 1 are the same however you reach the model. What changes is who runs each one. There are four common ways in, and a fifth that only the lab itself uses.

In a chat app the vendor runs nearly everything: the model, the safeguards, the harness and its instructions, and the computers the tools run on [10, 11]. You can still add standing instructions of your own and, in some apps, connect other services [67, 68]; on business plans, administrators set some of the controls.

In a coding assistant the vendor builds the harness, and you or your organization decides how it behaves: which actions run without asking, which need approval and which are blocked [4, 7, 8, 9, 13]. It can run on your own machine, with access to your files, or in the vendor’s cloud [4].

Through the API the vendor supplies the model and its safeguards. In most setups you build the harness and the place where the tools run, and any monitoring of your own runs is yours to add [14, 15, 16, 26, 65]. OpenAI’s documentation says it directly: “Add review and enforcement to your own harness” [14].

With open weights you download the model and run it on your own computers. The safety training comes with the weights, though whoever holds them can train it back out: one study weakened a model’s safety training with a handful of examples [69]. Everything else is yours to add.

In a lab’s own research run there is no customer. The lab trained the model, built the harness, set up the execution environment and chose which safeguards ran. The July 2026 incident happened in this mode: an OpenAI internal evaluation, run without production classifiers and with reduced safeguards [10, 11, 70, 71]. The next paper in this series covers it.

Key idea 7

Same parts, different owners.

The parts do not change with how you reach the model; who runs each one does. In a chat app the vendor runs almost all of it; through the API or with open weights, much of it is yours.

3 · One turn of the loop: the model proposes, the harness decides what runs

Every run is the same loop, repeated (Figure 3). The harness sends the model its context. The model answers with text, with a (a request to use a tool), or both [5]. With no tool call, the loop ends. With one, the harness checks it against the operator controls, in particular the (Anthropic calls it the permission mode): some actions run without asking, some wait for a person to approve them, and some are refused [8, 9, 14]. Some products add a , a second model that approves or blocks actions before they run [4, 7]. A permitted action runs in the execution environment, the result comes back, the harness adds it to the context, and the next turn begins [4, 5].

The division of labor is simple: the model proposes; the harness decides, runs and limits [5, 7, 14]. One pass from start to stop is a run; in a product, a session [4, 10].

Key idea 8

The model proposes; the harness decides.

Every action a model seems to take is the harness carrying out a request, within limits set by different people in different places.

BURSERA’S TAKE — “EDGE OF TOMORROW”Bursera’s own view — not the record

If you have not seen the 2014 film “Edge of Tomorrow”, starring Tom Cruise and Emily Blunt, it is worth a watch.

At its heart it is a time loop. The man caught in the loop, played by Tom Cruise, has every reason to keep going (the human race faces extinction) and lives the same day again and again, looking for a way to beat the attackers.

As the movie starts his character is arrested and wakes up to an angry sergeant on the last day before the assault on the beaches. That is where he wakes up each time the loop starts.

The man who wakes up that very first time is the raw model. He has a good general understanding of the world and of what he can do.

Each time the loop starts again, he “remembers” how he got there. He has the original context: the world he operates in, its challenges and how things work there. He has the new information from the latest loop. And he has everything from all the earlier loops: what worked, what didn’t, and what he learned. From all of it he decides (predicts) the next best move. At one point he meets another person who knows about the loop, and they become allies in solving the problem. That fits the model in general, and even the Hugging Face incident, where the agents ran into other agents and found ways to work together.

The difference is that the character has a brain and remembers. A model wakes up every time as it did the very first time. The harness saves the results of the earlier work, and when it wakes the model again, it hands over all of the history up to that point, every word of it, plus whatever new thing you have asked. Then the model, like Tom Cruise’s character, tries to do what you asked and predicts how to do it best. If the model is running agentically, it may be looping on its own. Like Tom Cruise’s character, it is sent back in with all the prior loops’ information and the only new thing being an instruction to try to do better.

When you or I are paying for our own use of managed AI solutions, the model won’t loop “forever” with so many resource requirements because it is very expensive. The harness for consumer products has limits to keep the tools from running up such a large bill. But in training and testing, models are given very few limits on resources, and they will bring the kind of commitment to success we see in Tom Cruise’s character.

Limits, and where they apply

Because every turn adds to the context, and the whole context is usually sent again on the next call, a run grows quickly [3, 5, 62, 72]. Several limits cap it. They sit in different places and are set by different people. The operator is whoever sets up a deployment: the vendor in its own chat app, or you or your organization when you build on the API or configure a coding assistant. The operator controls in Figure 1 are that operator’s approval settings; the turn and spending limits below are other settings the same operator chooses, held in the harness. The Engine and the Car

The large language model at the heart of ChatGPT or Claude works like an engine, and it is built in two stages. First it is cast. The model is run over a huge library of text and adjusted again and again so that it gets better at predicting the next piece of text. This gives it raw power: it can produce fluent writing, working code and step-by-step answers, but it has no particular aim. Then it is tuned. People rate its answers, written guidelines are applied, and the model is adjusted further so that it follows instructions, tries to be helpful and declines certain requests. When tuning ends, the engine is sealed. Its internal settings (the “weights”) stay fixed while you use it, and nothing you type in a chat changes them. Each reply is the engine producing one piece of text at a time, based only on what has been put in front of it.

An engine on a test stand goes nowhere. What you actually use is the car the company builds around it, and much of what the car can do is decided there. The maker’s hidden instructions (the “system prompt”) set the route and the driving style before you type a word. The windshield is the “context window”: the engine sees only the current conversation plus whatever files, search results or saved memory notes the car puts in front of it. That is how a product can seem to remember you even though the model itself has not changed. The wheels and attachments are tools such as web search, running code, reading files or operating a computer, and the engine reaches the road only through the tools the car connects. The brakes are separate safety checks that screen what goes in and comes out, plus permission settings that decide which actions need a person’s approval. Put the same engine in a different car (a chat app, a coding assistant, or another company’s product built on the developer API) and it will behave differently, because the car decides what the engine sees, what it can touch and what stops it. So when one of these systems does something surprising, “what is the model like?” is only half the question. The other half is how the car was built and who set the controls.

BURSERA’S TAKE — NEW RISKS, FAMILIAR TERRITORYBursera’s own view — not the record

Those of us who use LLM tools and agents from third parties for normal work have a responsibility to understand them: what they are good at, where they are weak, and where their limits are. With them we can produce good work or bad work. We can accidentally take down our own systems, and possibly a partner’s.

I see these risks as very close to the ones we take when a group of young developers builds a serious system, or runs and supports the most critical systems in the business. If the inexperienced are led by people who understand what the tools are good and bad at, with good processes for how the work is done and tested, the risks are manageable. If the boundaries are clear and enforced, between where inexperienced people work and where senior people work, and between what each role may touch, the reward is worth the risk. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Microsoft have built harnesses and frameworks to reduce that risk, and it is their job to provide models that are constrained from creating havoc for responsible organizations.

Training and testing new models is a very different situation. Training gives models the hardest challenges and the most room to try things, to see how they handle them. The organizations that do it need to be held to the highest standard.

It is like the early days of steam engines. The companies running them were blowing things up all the time, until regulation came along and enforced standards against reckless practices and the harm they caused. The invention of the steam engine was positive, but figuring it out and using it widely was incredibly dangerous. In the first decade of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers, founded in 1880, more than 2,000 boilers exploded. In 1905 a boiler explosion at a shoe factory in Brockton, Massachusetts, killed 58 people, and public outcry followed. In 1914 ASME published its first Boiler Code, the start of the rules and inspections boilers run under today [77].

My take: as we work out how to use these tools in our work, and how to regulate them so as to lower risk and cause less disruption and harm, it helps to understand the parts and what each one does, and to recognize that the risks and the responsibilities differ with how the tools are used.

How it began. This paper was planned as one short part (about 2,400 words) of a series mostly about analysing the July 2026 OpenAI and Hugging Face incident: it was to explain how a running AI system is put together, so the incident papers could use it as their framework. It grew into a large piece of work in its own right and became the only paper in the series’ first release. Claude Fable 5.1, an Anthropic model, wrote the first draft in early November 2026 from an evidence pack that a Claude Sonnet 5.5 helper assembled for this paper; a separate Claude Opus 5.5 session that had not seen the drafting checked it item by item, and Fable revised it once. The evidence pack drew on the project’s register of checked incident facts, its shared vocabulary for the parts of an AI system, research notes on how models are trained and run, and word-for-word copies of OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s developer documentation. Sourced from: The project’s claim register: incident facts, each tied to a primary source such as OpenAI’s incident technical report, METR’s report and Hugging Face’s posts (the incident facts the paper describes when a lab runs the model itself); The project’s shared vocabulary for the parts of an AI system (the layer model and the terms: model, harness, context, tools, execution environment, controls, governance); Research notes on how models are trained and run, gathered by several AI research engines and subagents, merged and checked against papers and vendor documentation (how models are trained and run, as leads checked against papers and vendor documentation); Word-for-word copies of OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s developer documentation, captured October 2026 (the documented behaviour of harnesses, tools, sandboxes and limits). (origin: fresh)
